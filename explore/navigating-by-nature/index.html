Chapter 1 · The Horizon

How does the Hawaiian star compass work?

The navigator divides the whole horizon into 32 equal sections called houses. The sun, moon and every star rise in a fixed house on the eastern horizon and set in the mirror house on the western horizon, so watching where a body rises tells you where it will set, and that alone holds a course for a day.

Four cardinal houses stand alone: north, east, south and west. Each quarter between them is split again and again into seven more houses, and the four quarters carry the names of their winds. The compass is not a thing the navigator holds; it is a thing the canoe stands inside, with the navigator reading it against the rising and setting of the sky.

Chapter 2 · The Stars

Which star tells a navigator they have reached Hawaiʻi?

Hōkūleʻa, the star Western astronomy calls Arcturus, passes directly overhead at the latitude of Hawaiʻi. Every star rises in its own compass house and sets in the mirror house, night after night; a fixed star's height above the horizon tells the navigator how far north or south they have come.

In the northern sky Polaris holds its place while everything wheels around it. In the south the Cross stands low and turns through the night. Between the two, the whole sky reports latitude. The sky in the scene above is computed for the date and place you set, from the positions of the brightest stars; change the place to Tahiti and Polaris drops below the horizon while the Cross climbs.

Chapter 3 · The Hand

How do Polynesian navigators find latitude with their hand?

With the arm held straight, the hand becomes a ruler against the horizon. A navigator knows the angular width of each finger and reads a star's altitude in finger widths; a fixed star's altitude above the horizon is the observer's latitude. No sextant, no instrument at all.

Hold the same star at the same height night after night and the canoe is sailing a line across the ocean. Every navigator learns the measure of their own hand, and that measure travels with them.

Chapter 4 · Swell and Wind

How do wayfinders navigate when clouds hide the stars?

They read the ocean. A long swell is born in a storm far over the horizon and travels in a straight line for days, from one compass house to its opposite. The trade wind holds a steady house for days at a time. A navigator feels each swell through the hull as a pitch or a roll and keeps the angle between them to hold the course.

Two swells crossing give two directions to hold. The wind on the cheek gives a third. And at dusk seabirds fly home: follow them, and there is land.

Chapter 5 · The Wake

How did Polynesians measure speed and distance at sea?

By counting the wake. Timing how long foam takes to pass from bow to stern gives the canoe's speed. Speed, heading, point of origin and elapsed time together give position by dead reckoning, carried in the navigator's head across three thousand miles of open ocean.

Where you started. Which way you are pointed. How fast. How long. That is the whole sum, and it is still how a navigator on Hōkūleʻa finds an island today.
